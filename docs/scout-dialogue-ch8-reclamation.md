## 1. District Intro

**`district_intro`**

> Welcome to The Reclamation. This is the endgame.
>
> You've secured your accounts. You've hardened your devices. You've learned to spot the scams. Now you learn who's been selling your data this whole time — and you take it back.
>
> The data broker industry is worth over $200 billion a year. That's not a typo. Two hundred billion dollars generated by buying and selling information about people — about you — without your meaningful consent. Your name, your address, your phone number, your employer, your salary, your relatives, your browsing habits, your purchase history, your GPS coordinates as you move through your day. All of it packaged, priced, and sold.
>
> This district is different from the others. You're not securing an account or hardening a device. You're reaching into the machinery that profits from your existence and pulling yourself out, one broker at a time. Some of them will make it easy. Some of them will make it deliberately hard. Some of them will re-add you from public records within six months. That's why the organizations fighting this fight matter — they're building the legal infrastructure to make this permanent.
>
> The supply chain has layers. People-search sites are the retail end — what anyone with a web browser can find. Enterprise aggregators are the wholesale end — what corporations buy. Ad networks track what you do online. Location brokers track where you physically go. And underneath all of it, your credit file determines whether you can rent an apartment, get a loan, or open a bank account. We start there.

## 2. Facility Intros

### Credit Bureau Freeze

**`facility_intro_credit_freeze`**

> A credit freeze is the single most effective identity theft prevention available to you. It's free, it takes five minutes per bureau, and it prevents anyone from opening credit in your name — no new credit cards, no loans, no accounts. The only friction: you temporarily unfreeze (also free, takes effect within an hour) when you apply for credit yourself. Before the Economic Growth Act of 2018, credit freezes cost $5-10 per bureau in most states. Consumer advocates fought for years to make them free. They won. Use what they won.

### People-Search Brokers

**`facility_intro_people_search`**

> Spokeo. Whitepages. BeenVerified. TruePeopleSearch. These are the retail layer of the surveillance economy. Type someone's name and get their address, phone number, email, relatives, and estimated income. The data comes from public records, purchased datasets, and the wholesale brokers further up the supply chain. Removing yourself from these sites is free, tedious, and impermanent — most will re-add you within 3-6 months from fresh public records. The game is attrition, not victory. You opt out, they re-add, you opt out again. That's why the California DELETE Act matters: one request to the DROP platform covers 600+ registered brokers, and the $200/day fines for non-compliance give the opt-out actual teeth.

### Enterprise Aggregators

**`facility_intro_enterprise_data`**

> This is the wholesale layer. People-search sites are the storefronts anyone can walk into. Enterprise aggregators are the warehouses behind them — and their customers are banks, insurers, employers, landlords, and law enforcement.
>
> LexisNexis has your CLUE report — your insurance claims history. Every fender bender, every water damage claim, every homeowner's insurance payout. Landlords check it before renting to you.
>
> Thomson Reuters CLEAR is the one investigators use. Skip tracers, private investigators, and law enforcement. A $27.5 million class-action settlement forced them to launch an opt-out page. Before that, there was no way out.
>
> Equifax Workforce Solutions — also called The Work Number — is the one that surprises people. Your employer reports your paycheck to them every pay period. Your salary, your job title, your dates of employment. Landlords and lenders buy this to verify your income. You didn't sign up for this. Your employer did it automatically.
>
> These companies have more data on you than any people-search site. The people-search sites are the retail packaging. These are the raw material.

### Ad & Tracking Networks

**`facility_intro_ad_trackers`**

> People-search brokers sell your identity — name, address, phone, relatives. Ad brokers sell your behavior — what you search, what you buy, what you read, what you click. They build profiles from your browsing history, app usage, purchase records, and loyalty program data, then sell those profiles to advertisers and anyone else who pays.
>
> Acxiom — now rebranded as LiveRamp — has profiles on 2.5 billion consumers worldwide. That is not a metaphor for "a lot." That is the literal number they report to investors. Two and a half billion individual behavioral profiles maintained by a single company most people have never heard of.
>
> The good news: bulk opt-out tools exist. The NAI Consumer Opt-Out covers 100+ ad networks in one click. The DAA WebChoices tool does the same for interest-based ads. And Global Privacy Control — a browser signal supported by Firefox, Brave, and DuckDuckGo — tells every website you visit that you opt out of data sale and sharing. Under California law, businesses must honor it.

### Location Data Brokers

**`facility_intro_location_brokers`**

> This is the layer that crosses the line from commercial surveillance into something darker.
>
> Every time an ad loads on your phone, your precise GPS coordinates are broadcast to hundreds of companies in a real-time auction that takes 100 milliseconds. This is called the ad bidstream. The winning bidder gets to show you an ad. But every company that participated in the auction — and lost — got your location data for free. They keep it.
>
> SafeGraph collects foot-traffic data from phone location. They sold it to the CDC during COVID for contact tracing. They also sold it to hedge funds for market intelligence. Same data, different buyer.
>
> X-Mode Social bought location data from Muslim prayer apps and sold it to U.S. military contractors. That sentence is not hyperbole. It is a factual description of what was documented and reported.
>
> Near Intelligence sold location data of people visiting Planned Parenthood clinics to anti-abortion groups. They filed for bankruptcy in 2024 after it was exposed.
>
> Gravy Analytics was breached in January 2025, exposing location data from millions of phones. The data had been collected via the ad bidstream — the same system that loads the ads on your weather app.
>
> The FTC has started to act. In 2024-2025, they banned Venntel/Babel Street and X-Mode/Outlogic from selling sensitive location data. But the ad bidstream itself is still running. Your defense is on your phone: Settings, Privacy, Location Services. Set most apps to "While Using" or "Never." Deny location to apps that have no reason to know where you are.

### Government ID Defense

**`facility_intro_govt_id_defense`**

> Your driver's license and Social Security number are not just cards in your wallet. They're sitting in databases at every service that ever asked you to "verify your identity" — banks, employers, landlords, car rental companies, crypto exchanges, dating apps, gig platforms, background check companies, and the identity verification services those companies hire. When any one of those gets breached — and they do — attackers get a copy of your government ID. Unlike a password, you cannot change your face or your Social Security number. The defense is layering other verification on top: credit freezes prevent fraudulent credit applications, an IRS Identity Protection PIN prevents someone from filing taxes in your name, and an SSA Self Lock prevents electronic access to your Social Security record.

## 3. Lore Entries

### `lore_data_broker_economy`

> The data broker industry generates over $200 billion in annual revenue. For context, that's larger than the global music industry, the global video game industry, and the global newspaper industry combined. The product is you. Not the anonymized, aggregated version of you that companies describe in their privacy policies — the specific, identifiable, enriched version of you that includes your name, your home address, your employer, your salary, your purchase history, your health conditions, and your daily movement patterns. The reason you've never heard of most data brokers is that their customers are other companies, not consumers. You are not the customer. You are the inventory.

### `lore_delete_act`

> In 2023, California passed the DELETE Act — the first law to create a single mechanism for opting out of all registered data brokers at once. The California Privacy Protection Agency (CPPA) built the DROP platform, which processes one deletion request across 600+ registered brokers. $200/day fines per unfulfilled request. The CPPA has a dedicated enforcement strike force — nine enforcement actions so far.
>
> Before this, opting out meant visiting each broker's website individually. Each one had its own form, its own process, its own verification requirements, and its own timeline. Some required you to mail a notarized letter. Some had opt-out pages with a "noindex" meta tag — deliberately hidden from search engines. Five companies removed those hidden tags after a Senate investigation exposed the practice.
>
> The DELETE Act doesn't solve the problem — brokers re-add you from fresh public records. But it changes the economics. The cost of compliance is now on them, not on you. That's a structural shift.

### `lore_exactis`

> In 2018, a security researcher discovered that Exactis — a data broker in Palm Coast, Florida — had left a database containing 340 million records exposed on the open internet. Not hacked. Not breached by sophisticated attackers. Left open. The database contained names, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, and hundreds of personal characteristics for nearly every American adult. Exactis effectively went dark after the exposure. No meaningful settlement was reached. No fine was paid. Your defense is downstream: the data was sold to other brokers before the exposure. Opt out of LexisNexis, Thomson Reuters, and Data Axle. Keep your credit frozen. Check HaveIBeenPwned.

### `lore_work_number`

> Equifax Workforce Solutions, also called The Work Number, maintains a database of employment and salary records for over 140 million Americans. Your employer almost certainly reports to it — automatically, every pay period. Your salary, your job title, your start date, your end date if you've left. Landlords buy this data to verify your income on rental applications. Lenders use it for loan underwriting. Background check companies include it in pre-employment screening. You did not consent to this. Your employer opted in — usually as part of their payroll processing agreement. Equifax charges others to access it and charges you nothing because you are the product, not the customer. You can freeze your Work Number file (separate from your credit freeze) and request your own report for free.

### `lore_thomson_reuters`

> Thomson Reuters CLEAR is a database used by law enforcement agencies, private investigators, skip tracers, and debt collectors. It aggregates data from public records, utility connections, and other data brokers into detailed profiles that can locate individuals. For years, there was no opt-out. Thomson Reuters took the position that the data was derived from public sources and therefore required no consent mechanism. A $27.5 million class-action settlement changed that. They now have an opt-out form. They did not build it because they wanted to. They built it because a court ordered them to. Your letter matters, even when it feels futile. Every unanswered opt-out request becomes evidence for the next enforcement case.

### `lore_bidstream`

> The real-time bidding system — the ad bidstream — is the hidden infrastructure underneath location data brokers. When an ad is about to load on your phone, the app sends a bid request to an ad exchange. The bid request contains your device's GPS coordinates, your device ID, the app you're using, and sometimes your age, gender, and interests. Hundreds of companies receive this bid request simultaneously. They have 100 milliseconds to bid. One of them wins and shows you the ad. Every other company that received the bid request got your location data and kept it — even though they didn't win the auction, and even though you never saw their ad. This happens every time any ad loads on your phone. Dozens of times per hour. The volume of location data generated by this system is staggering, and it is the source that feeds SafeGraph, Gravy Analytics, and the other location brokers. Your defense is reducing the number of apps that can include your location in bid requests: Settings, Privacy, Location Services, and deny location access to everything that doesn't need it.

### `lore_legal_victories`

> The resistance is winning. Not quickly, not completely, but the legal landscape has shifted dramatically in the last five years:
>
> **GDPR (2018)** — The EU's General Data Protection Regulation established the right to erasure. Companies must delete your data on request. Meta was fined 1.2 billion euros in 2023 for transferring EU data to the US without adequate protections.
>
> **California CCPA/CPRA (2020/2023)** — Gave Californians the right to know what data is collected, delete it, and opt out of its sale. Extended in 2023 with the CPRA, which created the CPPA — the first dedicated privacy enforcement agency in the US.
>
> **California DELETE Act (2023)** — One request to the DROP platform covers 600+ registered data brokers. $200/day fines per unfulfilled request.
>
> **FTC enforcement (2024-2025)** — Banned Venntel/Babel Street and X-Mode/Outlogic from selling sensitive location data. Required deletion of all previously collected data.
>
> **Vermont data broker registry** — First state to require data brokers to register publicly. Transparency as a tool — you can't regulate what you can't name.
>
> **Illinois BIPA** — The Biometric Information Privacy Act led to a $650 million settlement against Facebook for using facial recognition on photos without consent. The only state law that gives individuals a private right of action for biometric data violations.
>
> **EU Digital Markets Act (2024)** — Forced Apple to allow sideloading, forced Google to unbundle services, required interoperability between messaging platforms.
>
> None of these happened automatically. Every one was fought for by the organizations in the next section.
